Should every green light be equal?

I counted 3,122 cars at Lincoln Street and Green Bay Road, then built math and a camera-based control model to see how much waiting a smarter signal could remove.

The 30-second version

I started with a simple local question: if far more cars travel through an intersection in one direction, why should both directions get equal green time? So I stopped guessing. I went to a real Evanston intersection, counted traffic for an hour, and let the data decide.

From there I derived the best fixed timing with calculus, built a simulation where queues carry over from minute to minute, and designed a camera system that could feed a smarter controller. I also say plainly where the model falls short, because a result is only useful if you know its limits.

The problem

A signal has a limited amount of green to hand out. A 60/60 split is simple, but if one road carries almost three times the traffic of the other, drivers on the busy road pay for it. Even a well-tuned fixed plan can't see a backed-up line forming right now.

The key result of Phase I is clean: under the model, green time should be split in direct proportion to demand. With R as the North–South red time and L = 120 s:

R* = L · λEW / (λNS + λEW)  ⇒  G*NS = 120 × 0.7165 ≈ 85.98 s

Phase II: a signal that can see

Averages hide the moments that hurt most: a surge, a slow-clearing queue. In Phase II the controller updates every minute, carries queues forward in time, and weighs three things:

1 · Arrivals
Vehicles crossing a virtual line about 400 ft upstream.
2 · Queue
Vehicles waiting near the stop line, which persist into later minutes.
3 · Discharge
How quickly vehicles actually clear on green. Two slow periods showed up in my data.
Queued(t+1) = max(0, Queued(t) + Arrivalsd(t) − Servedd(t))

The arrival-only controller reacts to the newest counts. The camera-aware controller also sees inherited backlog and clearing speed, so a direction can earn extra green even when its latest arrivals are smaller.

The 400-ft zone is a design assumption, not an engineering standard. The field data were counted directly; the camera pipeline is the proposed way to automate it.

Vehicle-seconds. Average per vehicle: 337.3 s → 63.9 s → 44.5 s → 37.1 s.

The comparison that matters most is between the two adaptive controllers: same one-minute updates, same 25–95 s green limits, and the only difference is queue and discharge information. That is worth 16.77% less delay.

Phase I and Phase II measure different things (expected red delay vs. persistent queue delay), so their numbers shouldn't be compared directly.

How I did the work

  • Observe. One hour at Lincoln Street and Green Bay Road, starting around 8:00 AM, recording each vehicle by direction plus minute-level queue and discharge conditions.
  • Model. Derived the fixed split that minimizes expected red-signal delay, then compared it with a 60/60 baseline.
  • Simulate. Ran four strategies on identical minute-by-minute data so the comparison was fair, with controllers only allowed to use past information.
  • Stress-test. Tried nine synthetic road layouts, clearly labeled as hypothetical, and reported both relative and absolute effects.
  • Check myself. Listed the limitations and laid out a plan to compare against real timing plans in shadow mode, without touching the live signal.

Biggest lesson: a headline percentage can mislead. The queue trajectories, the assumptions behind them, and the absolute numbers matter just as much.

Why Evanston

Lincoln Street and Green Bay Road isn't a made-up intersection. The City's Green Bay Road Corridor Improvements Project includes traffic-signal improvements at Lincoln Street, part of a larger effort to improve safety for pedestrians, vehicles, buses, and trucks. This project is a transparent, student-scale model of that real local problem, not a replacement for the City's engineering.

What this does and doesn't show

  • One morning, one hour, one intersection. Repeating across days, seasons, and weather is the first next step.
  • Two-phase model: no turn phases, pedestrians, buses, or coordination with neighboring signals.
  • Discharge capacity is a simplified assumption, not lane-by-lane calibration.
  • The camera system isn't built or tested yet. Occlusion, night, weather, and detection error are open questions.

Next: replicate the counts, build the video pipeline and compare it to hand counts, then run the model in shadow mode against real timing plans.
